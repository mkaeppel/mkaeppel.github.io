Multimodal Investigations of Listening Effort in Children

Master's Thesis | RWTH Aachen University

View the Exposé on the Institute for Hearing Technology and Acoustics

Project Overview

The ability to understand speech and comprehend language plays an integral role in our communication, social interactions, and cognitive development. Especially in children, hearing and processing speech plays an important role in gaining knowledge but also in the development of linguistic and social skills. Speech processing is therefore not a purely sensory task; in fact, it results from an interaction between auditory perception, linguistic abilities, and cognitive resources such as attention and performance, which impact children’s development and mental health (Klatte et al., 2013; Shield and Dockrell, 2003; World Report on Hearing, 2021).

However, in real-world settings, especially in educational environments, the acoustic conditions are rarely ideal. Listening and paying attention to speech become challenging and demanding tasks. Background noise and multiple simultaneous speakers increase the load on the auditory system, causing cognitive, attentional, and perceptual costs of speech processing, which are commonly referred to as listening effort in the literature. Listening effort is typically measured using subjective questionnaires, behavioral measures based on single- and dual-task paradigms, and neurophysiological methods (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016).

Previous studies (Kemper et al., 2025; Prodi and Visentin, 2019; Wu et al., 2016) have shown that listening effort in adults does not increase linearly with task difficulty but follows a nonlinear curve that reaches a maximum at intermediate intelligibility levels and is reduced under very easy or very difficult listening conditions. While these studies were primarily focused on adults (Kemper et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2016) and those involving children related to different acoustic scenarios, such relationships in children have so far received little attention - particularly in very low speech intelligibility.

The goal of this work is to investigate listening effort across different levels of speech intelligibility in children aged ten to twelve. A core question is whether a nonlinear relationship between speech intelligibility and listening effort can be observed in neurophysiological and behavioral data, and how these measures compare to those of adults.

For data collection, a listening experiment specifically designed for children is used, in which different dimensions of listening effort under variable intelligibility levels are measured using self-report questionnaires, behavioral measures such as response time in a single-task paradigm following Seitz and Fels, 2025, and neurophysiological methods such as electroencephalography (EEG). Speech intelligibility is systematically adapted by varying the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) relative to the individual speech reception threshold (SRT). The analysis will focus on modeling the relationship between speech intelligibility and listening effort, including fitting non-linear functions to the behavioral and neurophysiological data such as the parietal alpha activity in the EEG.

The findings will provide a more differentiated understanding of listening effort in children and its relationship to speech intelligibility, and will extend the fundamental research on auditory processing and cognitive load beyond adult listeners. Furthermore, the results will inform practical applications, such as the acoustic design of listening environments for children and the enhancement of child-specific hearing technologies, both of which play a crucial role in children’s development and mental well-being.

The project is supervised by Julia Seitz, M.Sc. and Prof. Dr. Janina Fels
