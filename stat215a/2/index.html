Lab 2: Dialectometry and Linguistic Variation

Stat 215A - UC Berkeley | Fall 2025

Project Overview

This lab explores the geography of American English through computational dialectometry—a quantitative approach to studying how language varies across regions. Using data from a large-scale dialect survey with nearly 50,000 respondents across the United States, we investigate whether modern statistical methods can uncover meaningful regional linguistic patterns and assess the stability of these findings.

The dataset consists of responses to dozens of lexical questions (e.g., "What do you call a sweetened carbonated beverage?") paired with geographic coordinates. This structure allows us to explore three central questions: What broad geographic patterns exist? Can dimensionality reduction reveal underlying structure? How stable are the detected clusters under perturbations?

Data Cleaning and Preparation

Before analysis, substantial data cleaning was required. The original dataset contained 540 missing cities, 1,020 missing coordinates, and inconsistent formatting. We implemented a two-stage imputation strategy:

  • When location names were present but coordinates missing, we matched ZIP codes to their central coordinates
  • When coordinates existed but names were missing, we performed reverse geocoding using spatial joins

After cleaning, 47,317 complete records remained. Survey responses were aggregated into 1°×1° latitude-longitude grid cells to reduce overplotting and stabilize local variation while preserving regional patterns.

Dimensionality Reduction

Three complementary dimension reduction techniques were applied to visualize high-dimensional lexical variation:

  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Revealed broad continuous gradients. PC1 (4.0% variance) separates South from North/Midwest, while PC2 (2.8% variance) highlights East/West contrasts. PCA smooths regional patterns into interpretable axes of variation.
  • Nonnegative Matrix Factorization (NMF): Identified additive "dialect profiles" that emphasize localized clusters rather than opposing gradients. NMF components highlight the South, Midwest, and distinctive regional features like Wisconsin's "bubbler" usage, producing sharper geographic boundaries than PCA.
  • t-SNE: A nonlinear technique that preserves local neighborhood structure, revealing fine-grained clusters and subregional patterns. While t-SNE sharpens boundaries between dialect areas, it sacrifices global interpretability for local resolution.

These methods demonstrate that American English dialectal variation can be meaningfully summarized along a small number of dimensions, confirming known regional divisions (South, Midwest, Northeast) while also highlighting unexpected local phenomena.

Clustering Analysis

To partition the data into discrete dialect regions, three clustering approaches were compared:

  • K-means clustering: Produced interpretable geographic divisions for k=3 (South, Midwest, Northeast/West Coast) but was sensitive to initialization and sometimes created spurious isolated clusters at higher k values.
  • NMF-based clustering: Applied winner-takes-all assignment to NMF components, yielding clearer regional boundaries than k-means by leveraging additive dialect profiles. Clusters were more interpretable as they directly reflected underlying variant distributions.
  • Hierarchical clustering: Revealed the nested structure of linguistic similarity through dendrograms, showing how regions merge at different scales. Ward's method produced stable three-cluster solutions consistent with k-means and NMF, while avoiding isolated single-cell outliers.

Key Finding: All three methods consistently identified the same major dialect regions (South, Midwest, Northeast/West Coast), suggesting these patterns are robust features of the data rather than methodological artifacts. However, finer subdivisions and boundary locations varied across methods, indicating that dialect transitions are gradual continua rather than sharp lines.

Stability Analysis

To test robustness, k-means clustering (k=5) was re-run under two perturbations: changing random seeds and bootstrapping the dataset by subsampling 60-80% of grid cells. The major regional clusters remained stable across all perturbations, but boundary regions and smaller subdivisions showed variability.

This confirms that the core three-region structure (South, Midwest, Northeast/West Coast) is a genuine geographic signal, while finer distinctions should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This analysis demonstrates that large-scale dialect survey data combined with modern statistical methods can reliably recover known regional linguistic patterns. The consistent results across PCA, NMF, t-SNE, k-means, and hierarchical clustering provide strong evidence for stable dialect regions in American English. However, the gradual transitions between regions and sensitivity of fine-scale patterns to methodological choices underscore the continuum nature of linguistic variation.
