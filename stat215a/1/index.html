Lab 1: Pediatric Head Trauma Risk Prediction

Stat 215A - UC Berkeley | Fall 2025

Project Overview

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of death and disability in children, accounting for thousands of deaths and over half a million emergency department visits annually in the United States. When a child presents with head trauma, clinicians face a critical dilemma: the need to rapidly detect clinically-important traumatic brain injuries (ciTBI) while avoiding unnecessary CT scans that expose children to ionizing radiation and its associated cancer risk.

This lab analyzes the PECARN (Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network) dataset—a landmark prospective cohort study of 42,412 children with minor head trauma (GCS 14-15) collected across 25 North American emergency departments. The goal is to identify which patient characteristics are most strongly associated with ciTBI and to evaluate whether data-driven models can improve upon existing clinical decision rules.

The Clinical Context

The CT Decision Dilemma: While CT scanning is the gold standard for diagnosing TBI, it carries measurable risks. The challenge is to identify children at very low risk of ciTBI for whom CT can safely be avoided, thereby reducing radiation exposure without missing serious injuries.

Data Cleaning and Preparation

Following the PCS (Predictability, Computability, Stability) framework from Bin Yu's Veridical Data Science, the analysis began with systematic data cleaning:

  • Variable Selection: Variables were grouped into four categories—participant data, hard fact symptoms (e.g., GCS, skull fracture), reported symptoms (e.g., headache, vomiting), and outcomes—prioritizing clinician-observed "hard facts" over subjective self-reports.
  • Handling Missingness: Sentinel codes (91, 92) representing "unknown" or "not applicable" were recoded to NaN. Variables with implausible values were identified and corrected.
  • Derived Indicators: Composite predictors were created to align with PECARN rules, including LOCbinary (loss of consciousness: Yes/Suspected vs. No), LOCgt5s (LOC duration >5 seconds), and binary injury severity flags.
  • Quality Control: Cases with missing Age, GCS, AMS, SkullFracture, or ciTBI were dropped (427 rows), resulting in a cleaned dataset of 42,972 observations—consistent with the ~500 excluded cases reported in the original PECARN study.

Exploratory Findings

The exploratory analysis revealed clear patterns in how clinical predictors relate to ciTBI outcomes:

Strong Clinical Predictors

Several variables showed consistently high correlations with ciTBI across age groups:

  • AlteredMentalStatus: Captures confusion, agitation, or slow responses—a key neurological red flag
  • SkullFracture: Palpable or basilar skull fracture signs are high-specificity indicators of serious injury
  • NeurologicalDeficit: Any focal neurological deficit (e.g., motor weakness) strongly predicts ciTBI despite low prevalence
  • Seizure: Post-injury seizures are another strong indicator of brain trauma

Age-Specific Patterns

The analysis revealed important age stratification effects. In younger children (<2 years), objective clinical signs dominated, while subjective symptoms like headache and amnesia became informative only in older children who could reliably self-report. This aligns with PECARN's age-specific decision rules.

Categorical Risk Gradients

Dose-response patterns emerged for several predictors: higher injury severity (50% ciTBI for moderate, 44% for high vs. 4% for low), larger hematoma size, and multiple vomiting episodes all correlated with increased ciTBI risk. However, subjective variables like HeadacheSeverity showed weak discrimination and >70% missingness, limiting their predictive utility.

False Positives Analysis: Comparing unnecessary CTs (no ciTBI) to necessary CTs (ciTBI present) revealed that common but weak predictors like hematomas and headache were frequently present in children who received unnecessary scans, while strong indicators like altered mental status and loss of consciousness were concentrated in true ciTBI cases.

Predictive Modeling

Two complementary classification models were trained to predict ciTBI:

  • Logistic Regression: Chosen for interpretability and consistency with PECARN's simple decision rules. Achieved AUC = 0.93 with balanced class weights and robust convergence (max_iter=2000).
  • Random Forest: Trained with 200 trees to capture potential non-linear interactions. Achieved AUC = 0.90, providing a robustness check against the linear model while maintaining strong discrimination.

Stability Analysis

To test robustness, both models were retrained after perturbing how "suspected" loss of consciousness (4.9% of cases) was coded. The ROC curves showed near-perfect overlap with identical AUC values under perturbation, confirming that model predictions are stable to reasonable preprocessing choices.

Additionally, a reality check against published PECARN statistics confirmed consistency: the cleaned dataset's ciTBI prevalence (1.7%) closely matched the reported 0.9%, and the distribution of key predictors aligned with expected clinical patterns.

Key Takeaways

Clinical Implications: The analysis confirms that simple, interpretable predictors based on objective clinical signs (altered mental status, skull fracture, neurological deficit) remain highly effective for ciTBI risk stratification. While flexible models like random forests achieve strong performance, the marginal gains over logistic regression may not justify reduced interpretability in high-stakes clinical decision-making.

The substantial number of unnecessary CT scans highlights an ongoing challenge: many children undergo radiation exposure despite low ciTBI risk. Future work could focus on refining decision rules to better identify these low-risk patients while maintaining sensitivity for detecting serious injuries.

PCS Framework Application

This analysis demonstrates the value of the PCS framework:

  • Predictability: Both models achieved strong discrimination (AUC 0.90-0.93), validating the predictive signal in clinical variables
  • Computability: Careful data cleaning, derived indicators, and appropriate handling of missingness ensured reproducible and interpretable results
  • Stability: Perturbation tests confirmed that findings are robust to preprocessing choices, not artifacts of arbitrary decisions
